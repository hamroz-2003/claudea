The chair next to me stayed empty for the whole service. I kept looking at it, as if Claire might slip in late, the way she always did, smelling of rain and excuses.

She didn't come. Not to the church, not to the cemetery, not to the small lunch at Aunt Ruth's house where everyone whispered her name like it was a bad word.

"She could have called, at least," Ruth said, refilling my coffee. "Your mother asked for her. Every day, that last week."

I didn't answer. What was there to say? I had sat by Mom's bed for three months. Claire had sent one text. Thinking of you both.

Two weeks later, we sat in the lawyer's office. Claire came this time. She wore a gray coat and didn't take it off.

Mr. Hale opened a folder and cleared his throat. "Your mother left a letter," he said. "She asked me to read it before anything else."

I expected a list. The house, the car, the savings. Instead, he read my mother's words, slow and careful.

"Claire, I know why you stayed away. I never told your sister, because you asked me not to. But I won't carry it anymore."

Claire closed her eyes. And for the first time in years, my little sister started to cry.
